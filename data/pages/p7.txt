Laser peripheral iridotomy
An iridotomy is a small full-thickness opening in the peripheral iris that lets aqueous bypass the pupil. It
equalises the pressure in front of and behind the iris and relieves pupil block.

Indications
    After acute primary angle closure, in both eyes
    Primary angle closure and primary angle closure glaucoma
    Selected primary angle closure suspects. The ZAP and ANA-LIG trials showed only a small protective
    effect, so LPI is usually reserved for higher-risk people: symptoms of intermittent closure, a need for
    drugs that may cause pupil block, frequent dilated examinations (for example diabetic retinopathy or
    AMD), poor access to eye care, or a family history of angle closure.
    Pigment dispersion with marked reverse pupil block (less commonly used now)

Technique
Pre-treat with pilocarpine to stretch and thin the iris and with apraclonidine to reduce the post-laser
pressure spike. Place the opening in the peripheral iris, ideally in an iris crypt, either fully covered by the
upper lid or temporally, using a contact lens to focus the beam. Avoid the lid margin and tear meniscus,
where a partly covered opening is most likely to cause visual symptoms. The Nd:YAG laser is used most
often; argon laser can be added first in thick, dark irides to reduce bleeding and the energy needed.

Complications
    A temporary rise in IOP, so recheck the pressure after about an hour
    Bleeding from the iris or a small hyphaema, usually stopped by gentle pressure with the contact lens
    Anterior uveitis
    Visual disturbance such as lines, glare or ghost images, particularly if the opening is only partly
    covered by the lid
    Corneal epithelial or endothelial burns, especially in a shallow chamber
    Closure of the opening, needing repeat treatment
    Persistent angle closure despite a patent iridotomy, which suggests plateau iris or a lens mechanism
    Rarely, aqueous misdirection or damage to the lens or retina


Plateau iris
In plateau iris the ciliary processes are positioned anteriorly and hold the peripheral iris forward. The
central anterior chamber can look normal or only slightly shallow, while the iris surface is flat and the
peripheral angle is narrow. Patients tend to be younger than those with pure pupil block.

    Plateau iris configuration describes the anatomy seen on gonioscopy or imaging.
    Plateau iris syndrome describes an angle that stays closed, or closes again, despite a patent
    iridotomy. Darkness or dilation may be needed to show it. In the complete form the IOP rises; in the
    incomplete form the closure is less extensive and the IOP stays normal.
