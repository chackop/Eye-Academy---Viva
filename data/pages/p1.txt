Aqueous humour and intraocular pressure
Aqueous humour is secreted by the non-pigmented epithelium of the ciliary body, mostly by active
transport (with smaller contributions from ultrafiltration and diffusion), at roughly 2 to 3 microlitres per
minute. It passes from the posterior chamber through the pupil into the anterior chamber, then leaves
the eye by two routes.

    Conventional (trabecular) outflow. Aqueous passes through the trabecular meshwork into
    Schlemm's canal, then into collector channels and the episcleral veins. This route carries most of the
    outflow in adults and depends on pressure.
    Uveoscleral outflow. Aqueous passes through the ciliary muscle into the suprachoroidal space. This
    route is largely independent of pressure, is relatively larger in younger people, and is the main target
    of prostaglandin analogues.

Intraocular pressure (IOP) reflects the balance between production and outflow. In adults the population
average is about 15 to 16 mmHg, and 21 mmHg (about two standard deviations above the mean) is often
quoted as the upper limit of normal, but many people with glaucoma have pressures below this and many
people above it never develop damage. IOP varies through the day and is often highest in the early
morning.

Measuring IOP
Goldmann applanation tonometry at the slit lamp is the reference standard. It assumes an average
central corneal thickness (CCT): a thick cornea makes the reading higher than the true pressure, and a
thin cornea makes it lower. Record the time of measurement, the method and which eye. Rebound and
non-contact tonometers are useful for screening and for children but are less accurate at very high
pressures.
