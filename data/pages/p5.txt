Managing an acute attack
The aims are to lower the IOP quickly, relieve pupil block, protect the other eye and prevent a recurrence.
Treatment below is an outline only; doses and choices should follow local protocols.

Immediate treatment
    Position and support. Lie the patient flat (supine) to let the lens fall back, and give analgesia and an
    antiemetic.
    Topical IOP-lowering drops. A beta-blocker such as timolol 0.5% (unless the patient has asthma,
    COPD, bradycardia or heart block) and an alpha-agonist such as apraclonidine 1% or brimonidine
    0.2%. A topical carbonic anhydrase inhibitor can be added. Leave 3 to 5 minutes between drops.
    Prostaglandin analogues act too slowly to help in the acute phase.
    Topical steroid. Prednisolone acetate 1% to reduce inflammation.
    Pilocarpine. Pilocarpine 2% constricts the pupil and pulls the iris away from the angle. The iris
    sphincter is often ischaemic and does not respond while the IOP is very high, so pilocarpine works
    better once the pressure has started to fall (many protocols wait until the IOP is below 40 to 50
    mmHg). Avoid repeated intensive doses and strong concentrations such as 4%, which can push the
    lens-iris diaphragm forward and cause systemic toxicity.
    Fellow eye. Many units give pilocarpine 1% to the other eye until it has an iridotomy, although some
    specialists avoid this. Either way, the priority is prompt iridotomy in both eyes.
