Signs
    Reduced visual acuity, often 6/60 to hand movements
    Very high IOP, usually 50 to 80 mmHg (almost always above 40 mmHg)
    Ciliary injection (violaceous redness most marked around the limbus)
    Corneal epithelial oedema, often with a hazy view of the iris and fundus
    A shallow peripheral anterior chamber, usually with flare
    A fixed, mid-dilated, often vertically oval pupil, caused by ischaemia of the iris sphincter
    A hyperaemic, swollen optic disc if the fundus can be seen
    A narrow angle in the other eye, which is an important diagnostic clue. If the fellow angle is open, look
    hard for a secondary cause.
    After the attack: an initially low IOP (ciliary body shutdown), folds in Descemet membrane,
    glaukomflecken (small white anterior subcapsular lens opacities), spiral iris atrophy, an irregular or
    fixed pupil, posterior synechiae and PAS, and optic disc pallor with or without cupping

Triggers
Attacks are often triggered by things that dilate the pupil into the mid-position, where pupil block is
greatest.

    Dim light, such as a cinema or a dark room
    Emotional stress
    A semi-prone position, such as reading, which lets the lens move forward
    Dilating drops, including tropicamide and phenylephrine
    Drugs with anticholinergic effects, including some antihistamines (for example chlorphenamine),
    tricyclic antidepressants, antispasmodics, hyoscine travel-sickness patches and some inhaled
    bronchodilators (such as nebulised ipratropium)
    Sympathomimetic drugs, including decongestants and cold and flu remedies
    Rarely, strong miotics, which can pull the lens-iris diaphragm forward

Drug-induced bilateral angle closure from topiramate and some sulfonamide-type drugs works by a
different mechanism and is covered under secondary angle closure.
