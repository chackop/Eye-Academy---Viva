Gonioscopy shows a "double hump" sign on indentation: a central hump from the iris over the lens and a
peripheral hump from the iris over the ciliary processes. Ultrasound biomicroscopy confirms the anterior
ciliary processes. Laser iridoplasty is the procedure of choice when the angle stays closed after
iridotomy; other options are long-term pilocarpine and lens extraction.


Secondary angle closure
Secondary angle closure has an identifiable cause other than primary anatomy. It can be divided into
causes that push the iris forward from behind and causes that pull it into the angle from in front. It is
more often one-sided than primary angle closure.

Pushing mechanisms
    Phacomorphic or lens subluxation. A swollen cataractous lens, or a lens that has moved forward
    (for example after trauma, or in pseudoexfoliation or microspherophakia), pushes the iris forward.
    Treated by lowering the IOP and removing the lens.
    Ciliochoroidal effusion. Drugs such as topiramate, and some other sulfonamide-type drugs, cause
    fluid to collect around the ciliary body, which rotates forward. Typically both eyes are affected within
    the first two weeks of starting the drug, with a sudden myopic shift. Stop the drug (with the
    prescriber) and use cycloplegics, topical steroid and IOP-lowering drops. Avoid acetazolamide, which
    is itself a sulfonamide. Pilocarpine and iridotomy do not help, because there is no pupil block, and
    pilocarpine can make it worse. Effusions also occur after extensive panretinal photocoagulation, in
    nanophthalmos and with a central retinal vein occlusion.
    Aqueous misdirection (malignant glaucoma). Usually after eye surgery, especially in short eyes
    with angle closure, aqueous flows backwards into the vitreous and pushes the lens and iris forward.
    Both the central and peripheral chamber become flat despite a patent iridotomy. Treated with
    cycloplegics, aqueous suppressants and hyperosmotics, avoiding miotics; then, if needed, Nd:YAG
    laser to the anterior hyaloid face (in pseudophakic eyes), cyclodiode or vitrectomy.
    Posterior segment causes, such as large tumours, haemorrhage, a large serous retinal detachment,
    expanding gas or silicone oil after vitrectomy, or a tight scleral buckle.

Pulling mechanisms
    Neovascular glaucoma. A fibrovascular membrane grows over the angle after retinal ischaemia, for
    example from diabetes or central retinal vein occlusion. The angle is open at first, then the membrane
    contracts to zip the angle shut. Treat the ischaemia (anti-VEGF injections and panretinal
    photocoagulation) as well as the IOP.
    Uveitis. Inflammation leads to PAS, often inferiorly, and posterior synechiae can cause pupil block
    and iris bombé.
    Iridocorneal endothelial (ICE) syndrome, in which abnormal corneal endothelium migrates over the
    angle, often producing high PAS that reach beyond Schwalbe's line.
    Epithelial or fibrous downgrowth, after surgery or a penetrating injury.
